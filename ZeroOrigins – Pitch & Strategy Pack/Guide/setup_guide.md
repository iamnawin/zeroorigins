# Setup Guide — How to Use This Pack

*Read this before you start customizing templates.*
*It will save you time and prevent the most common mistakes.*

---

## 1. The Right Mindset Going In

Most people open a template pack and immediately start filling in blanks.
That's the wrong move.

Templates are scaffolding. Your job is to build the structure with your own material.

If you send a proposal that still feels like a template — you'll lose deals to someone
who sounds like they actually know the client's world.

**The goal is not to complete a document. The goal is to win the project.**

Everything in this pack is designed to help you do that.

---

## 2. How to Customize the Documents

### Step 1: Understand what the document is doing before you edit it

Every section in every template exists for a reason. Before you delete or rewrite a section, understand why it's there. The section notes in the templates explain this.

### Step 2: Fill in every bracket completely

Search for `[` in any document. Every bracketed item is a required input.
A proposal sent with `[CLIENT NAME]` still in it signals carelessness — and kills deals.

### Step 3: Write in your own voice

Replace the placeholder text with language that sounds like YOU.
The templates give you structure. The voice should be yours.

If your natural communication style is direct and punchy — write that way.
If you're more warm and conversational — write that way.
Professional doesn't mean robotic.

### Step 4: Remove what doesn't apply

Not every section in every template is relevant to every project.
Trim anything that doesn't add value for this specific client or engagement.
A focused proposal beats a comprehensive one.

### Step 5: Tailor Section 2 of the proposal (Understanding Your Situation)

This is the most important section.
Read your discovery notes. Then write 2–4 sentences that show you actually listened.
Reference specifics: problems they mentioned, numbers they shared, timelines they named.

If this section could apply to any client, rewrite it until it can't.

## 3. How to Pitch Clients

### The proposal is the SECOND step, not the first

Never send a proposal cold. Always run a discovery call first (use the discovery questions template).

A proposal without a conversation is a quote. A quote is easy to compare and reject.
A proposal that follows a real conversation is a recommendation — much harder to dismiss.

### The discovery call is where you win

Ask questions. Listen more than you talk. Take notes.
Show genuine curiosity about their business.

The client decision often happens on the call, not when they read the proposal.
Your job on the call is to establish trust and demonstrate competence.
The proposal just confirms what they've already decided.

### Follow up — always

Most freelancers send a proposal and go silent.
Follow up once, 2–3 days after sending:

> "Hi [NAME], just checking in on the proposal I sent on [DATE].
> Happy to answer any questions or jump on a quick call.
> What's your current thinking?"

That single follow-up wins more deals than any other tactic.

### Deliver the proposal within 48 hours

After a discovery call, the client's enthusiasm is highest in the next 24–48 hours.
A week-old proposal lands cold.

Aim: send within 24 hours. Maximum: 48 hours.

## 4. How to Structure Pricing

### Price the outcome, not the task

Clients don't pay for "10 hours of design work."
They pay for "a website that converts visitors into leads."

Frame your pricing in terms of what they get, not what you do.

### Use three tiers when possible

Give clients three options. Label them so the middle one looks obvious:

- **Basic / Starter** — Core deliverable only
- **Standard / Professional** — Core + the thing they actually want (this is the one you want them to pick)
- **Premium / Full Service** — Everything + extras + priority

Most clients pick the middle. The top tier makes the middle feel reasonable.
The bottom tier makes the middle feel safe.

### Never justify your price unprompted

State the price. Stop talking. Let the client respond.

If they push back on price, ask:
> "What number were you expecting? Help me understand where you're at."

This gives you data to work with instead of a guessing game.

### Know your minimum before every conversation

Before every sales call, decide:
*What is the minimum I'll accept for this project?*

If the deal comes in below that, walk away.
A bad deal costs you more than no deal: time, energy, and the opportunity cost of better clients.

### Anchor your pricing to market rates

Research what others at your level charge for similar work.
Your goal is to be competitive but not cheap.

Being the cheapest option attracts the worst clients.
A premium price signals that your work is worth protecting.

## 5. How to Avoid Confusion in Proposals

### The most common reasons proposals fail

1. **Vague scope** — client doesn't know what they're getting, so they don't sign
2. **No clear outcome** — the proposal lists tasks, not results
3. **Long and unfocused** — client skims, gets confused, passes
4. **No clear next step** — the proposal ends without telling them what to do
5. **Wrong tone** — too formal, too casual, or doesn't match their culture

### Fixes for each

**For vague scope:** Use the scope document template. Define deliverables precisely.

**For no clear outcome:** Add a "What success looks like" section (already in the proposal template). Fill it with specific, measurable results.

**For long and unfocused:** Edit ruthlessly. If a section doesn't help the client decide, cut it.

**For no clear next step:** End every proposal with a clear, single action:
"Reply to this email with any questions, or reply 'I'm in' and I'll send the invoice."

**For wrong tone:** Read the proposal out loud. Would you say this in a real conversation?
If not, rewrite it.

## 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Sending a proposal before running a discovery call
- Using generic language that could apply to any client
- Leaving placeholder text in the document
- Pricing without knowing your minimum number
- Failing to follow up after sending
- Not attaching a scope document (this is where disputes start)
- Sending a scope change verbally instead of in writing
