# Client Red Flags

*How to identify difficult clients before they cost you time, money, and sanity.*

---

## THE RULE

Every difficult client gives you signals before you sign the contract.
The problem is that when we're hungry for revenue, we ignore those signals.

This document is your permission to walk away.

A bad client doesn't just cost you money. They cost you:
- Time you could spend on a good client
- Energy and mental bandwidth
- Reputation (bad projects produce bad portfolio work)
- Confidence

Read this before every sales call and proposal.

---

## RED FLAGS ON THE FIRST CALL

### 1. "My last designer / developer / consultant was terrible."

What it signals: They may be the problem, not the vendor.
Everyone has one bad experience. A pattern of bad experiences points to an unrealistic client.

**What to do:** Ask: "What specifically went wrong?" If the answer is vague or blames the entire working relationship, be careful. If they can identify a specific, fixable failure — that's a better sign.

---

### 2. They can't clearly explain what they want.

What it signals: The project isn't ready. Or they haven't committed to a direction yet.

**What to do:** Run the discovery call properly. If after 30 minutes you still don't know what success looks like — the client hasn't defined it yet. Don't write a proposal until they do.

---

### 3. They want to see "a few options" before they decide anything.

What it signals: They want free strategy work. They'll take your ideas and possibly use them without hiring you.

**What to do:** A brief discovery call is fair. A detailed proposal with multiple directions before agreement is free consulting. Charge a paid discovery session if they want in-depth options before committing.

---

### 4. "Can you just ballpark it?"

What it signals: They're comparing you to competitors on price alone, or they don't understand the scope enough to have a real conversation.

**What to do:** A rough range is fine if scope is genuinely unclear. But if they push for a number before a proper conversation — they're shopping on price. Decide if that's a game you want to play.

---

### 5. They're in a rush but also vague about what they need.

What it signals: Urgency + vagueness = pressure to commit before you have enough information. The scope will expand once you're in.

**What to do:** Slow down. A good project takes the time it takes. "We need this by Friday" is only okay if the scope is completely clear and the price reflects the rush.

---

## RED FLAGS IN THE PROPOSAL STAGE

### 6. They want a lower price but won't reduce scope.

What it signals: They don't understand or respect the relationship between price and work.

**What to do:** Clearly link price to scope. "To bring the price down to X, we'd need to remove Y. Here's what that would look like." If they want the full scope at a lower price — that's a non-negotiation.

---

### 7. They take more than a week to respond to your proposal without explanation.

What it signals: Either the project isn't a real priority, or there are multiple decision-makers making it complicated, or they're stalling while shopping elsewhere.

**What to do:** Follow up once. Then let it go. Chasing is a bad sign for the rest of the project.

---

### 8. They push back on the scope document or contract terms.

What it signals: They expect to be able to add work freely. They've been burned by contracts in the past (possibly because they burned a vendor).

**What to do:** You can discuss terms. But never start without a scope document and written agreement. A client who resists this is telling you they plan to add work without paying for it.

---

### 9. "We don't need all that legal stuff, we trust each other."

What it signals: The exact opposite. People who operate in good faith don't have a problem with written agreements.

**What to do:** Non-negotiable. Written agreement before work begins. Every time. The agreement protects both of you.

---

### 10. They've already had 3 other agencies/freelancers quote the work.

What it signals: They're stuck in an evaluation loop. Either they're shopping on price or they can't make decisions.

**What to do:** Ask: "What's stopping you from making a decision?" If the answer is price — you can discuss. If the answer is unclear — walk away. Being the 4th quote is a bad starting position.

---

## RED FLAGS AFTER SIGNING

### 11. They miss the first payment.

What it signals: Cash flow issues, disorganization, or they're already testing what they can get away with.

**What to do:** Chase once professionally. Delay start until payment is received. Work does not begin without deposit — period.

---

### 12. They send feedback from multiple people with conflicting opinions.

What it signals: No internal decision-maker. You'll be stuck revising to please different stakeholders indefinitely.

**What to do:** Ask them to consolidate feedback to a single point of contact. Put this in your kickoff document. If it continues — escalate to the decision-maker directly.

---

### 13. They expand scope and expect it to be included.

What it signals: They don't respect the contract or the scope document.

**What to do:** Issue a change order immediately. Do not do extra work and "see if they notice." Document everything in writing.

---

### 14. They ghost during the project, then rush you at the end.

What it signals: Disorganized, not invested in the project, or managing you rather than collaborating.

**What to do:** Build feedback deadlines into your contract. If feedback is late, delivery shifts. This must be in writing before work starts.

---

### 15. Every deliverable is "not quite right" without specific feedback.

What it signals: They don't know what they want, or they're using revision rounds as therapy to figure it out.

**What to do:** When feedback is vague, ask specifically: "What would 'right' look like? Give me one concrete example." If they can't — the problem isn't your work.

## THE MOST EXPENSIVE CLIENTS

*These client types consistently produce the lowest ROI:*

| Client Type | Why They're Costly |
|------------|-------------------|
| "I just need something small and simple" | Nothing is ever small or simple once you're in |
| "My nephew could do this for free" | Doesn't value the work |
| "We can't pay much now but there's loads of work coming" | There won't be |
| "Previous person did this for half the price" | And it showed |
| "Let's see how this goes and decide next steps" | They want optionality at your expense |
| "I need it by Monday" (on Friday) | Emergency rate applies. If they balk, walk. |
| "We want full ownership of everything" (before discussing price) | Red flag on IP expectations |

## HOW TO WALK AWAY PROFESSIONALLY

Saying no is a skill. You can walk away without burning a bridge.

**Script for a bad-fit client:**

> "Thank you for the conversation — I appreciate you sharing the details.
> After reviewing the project, I don't think I'm the best fit for what you need right now.
> I'd recommend [ALTERNATIVE] for this type of work.
> Wishing you all the best with the project."

Short. Professional. No over-explanation.

---

## THE FLIP SIDE — WHAT GREEN FLAGS LOOK LIKE

Not all signals are bad. Here's what a great client looks like:

- ✓ Has done their research before the call
- ✓ Can articulate their problem clearly
- ✓ Has a decision-maker on the call
- ✓ Has a realistic budget and timeline
- ✓ Asks good questions about your process
- ✓ Doesn't push back on a written agreement
- ✓ Pays the deposit promptly
- ✓ Provides consolidated, specific feedback
- ✓ Respects your time and responds within agreed windows

When you find clients like this — keep them. Go above and beyond. Build the relationship.
The best source of new business is a happy existing client.

---

*Trust your gut. If something feels off on the first call, it will get worse, not better.*
*Your time is finite. Protect it.*
